If 2024 gave agents their plumbing, 2025 gave them their marketing. Almost every software product announced an "agent", and almost every company started a pilot.

The plumbing was real. Tool protocols spread quickly; by the end of the year Anthropic counted [more than 10,000 active public MCP servers](https://www.anthropic.com/news/donating-the-model-context-protocol-and-establishing-of-the-agentic-ai-foundation). Agents could now reach real systems. Whether they should, and whether they paid for themselves, was a different matter.

In June, Gartner [predicted that over 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-predicts-over-40-percent-of-agentic-ai-projects-will-be-canceled-by-end-of-2027), citing escalating costs, unclear business value and inadequate risk controls, and warned about "agent washing": existing chatbots and automation rebranded as agents [[1]](#source-1).

Even the productivity case turned out to be harder to measure than expected. A randomised study by METR found that [experienced open-source developers took 19% longer on real tasks when allowed to use AI tools, while believing they had been sped up](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/). METR has since marked those early-2025 results as out of date, which is the point: claims about agents age quickly and need re-measuring.

The pattern that held up was narrower. Agents did well on well-bounded jobs with clear inputs, cheap mistakes and a human who checked the result. They struggled where the job was vague, the data was messy, or nobody could say what "done" looked like.

## What to do about it

Before the next pilot, write down three things: the task and its owner, what a wrong action costs, and how you will measure value against today's process. Kill the pilots that cannot answer.
